We study ambulance routing jointly with selective signal priority, measuring both emergency travel and ordinary-traffic aftereffects. A supplied mesoscopic experiment is independently reproduced, corrected to enforce shared ambulance admission, storage and discharge constraints, and rerun for 14,400 policy episodes. A separate Eclipse SUMO implementation evaluates finite route–action choices under delayed noisy counts, lawful signal transitions and repeated missions. Its selective controller uses a queue-inspired burden score and an irreversible switch to aggressive joint control when observed free-flow progress deteriorates. Four elementary results specify restricted-residence accounting, spatial and individual positive effects, and the scope of the computed time band. In 1,728 microscopic evaluation runs, balanced common-horizon restricted ambulance duration was 401.57 s under route-only control, 211.19 s under aggressive joint control and 222.29 s under the selective controller. The selective-minus-aggressive difference was 11.10 [9.47, 12.94] s; positive spatial exposure changed by -3.84 [-4.73, -2.95] vehicle-hours and positive individual restricted residence by -1.64 [-2.14, -1.16] vehicle-hours. Intervals are 95 percent seed-block bootstrap intervals. The combined 20 s time-tolerance and reduced-spatial-exposure target was supported. The balanced fraction exceeding 300 s increased by 2.55 percentage points [1.42, 3.77]. Two selective-policy trips nevertheless lasted 1,812.5 and 2,050 s. All comparisons are synthetic, and corridor calibration remains outstanding. The study provides an executable method, auditable traffic accounting and a falsifiable evaluation, rather than a globally optimal or field-validated city controller.
