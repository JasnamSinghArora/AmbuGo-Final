# Verification and mesoscopic results

## Reproduction and statistical reconstruction

The supplied archive contains 14,400 evaluation rows covering 72 load–yielding–policy cells, with 200 seeds per cell and no duplicate cell–seed keys. Dispatch, origin and incident indicators match within each policy comparison. We independently reran all six policies for seeds 0, 37 and 199 at every load and yielding condition, producing 216 recomputed policy runs. No mismatch was found in the checked ambulance times, ordinary and person residence, wait summaries, unfinished populations or baseline-paired differences, using numerical tolerances of 10^−9 absolute and 10^−12 relative. This establishes reproduction of the supplied implementation on a stratified subset; it does not establish physical validity.

Independent statistical reconstruction recovered all 72 main-table rows and 48 policy-contrast rows to their reported rounding. The ordinary paired residence differences agreed with subtraction of the corresponding raw policy and baseline totals to within 5.4×10^−14 vehicle-hours. Tail-event counts matched exactly, and the Clopper–Pearson intervals agreed to rounding. The archived bootstrap and Holm calculations were numerically reproducible. We nevertheless distinguish numerical reconstruction from validity of an inferential interpretation: a sign-flip procedure requires sign exchangeability or an appropriate randomized assignment argument, which common random numbers alone do not provide. The revised results therefore emphasize effect estimates and paired scenario-level bootstrap intervals, whose finite-sample coverage is approximate.

## Mechanical verification and corrected reanalysis

The eight supplied verification checks were reproduced as passing, including 24 stress simulations with 43,200 audited ticks. Additional adversarial tests showed why this was insufficient to establish the advertised ambulance storage and capacity invariants. A link filled to 2.0 PCU accepted a 1.5-PCU ambulance and reached 3.5 PCU without an audit violation. An ambulance crossed an approach with zero discharge capacity, again without an audit violation. The original audit explicitly exempted ambulance occupancy above storage, and the discharge rule exempted ambulance PCU credit. A separate blocked-link example had an unfinished stop-line wait of 348 s while the reported maximum completed wait and over-cap count were both zero. The latter demonstrates the censoring limitation of the wait metric, rather than a new storage defect.

After correcting source admission, strict receiving storage and shared ambulance PCU discharge, 18 focused and stress checks passed. These include a full origin link that delays ambulance insertion by 43 s, a zero-capacity incident after ambulance admission that prevents discharge, strict ordinary conservation while an ambulance waits outside the network, and six high-load network stress runs. These tests address the identified mechanics. They do not validate physical yielding, lane allocation, pedestrian trajectories or real-city traffic behavior. The wait metric retains its original definition as an over-threshold completed discharge episode; unfinished waits are not silently reclassified as completed observations.

We then executed all 14,400 mechanically corrected policy runs with the original tuning fixed. All corrected ambulance outcomes were finite in the resulting analysis. The retained controller definitions, route assignment, signal design, incident process and yielding abstraction make this a matched sensitivity experiment. It is not a new independent confirmation of a preregistered performance claim.

## Ambulance benefit and ordinary residence

In the corrected no-yielding condition, phase-skipping preemption M2 retained a substantial ambulance benefit. At nominal load target 0.60, mean travel fell from 357.61 s under M0 to 204.17 s under M2. The paired difference was −153.44 s, with a 95 percent bootstrap interval [−162.22, −144.96]. At target 0.80, the means were 433.35 and 252.81 s, with difference −180.55 s [−192.79, −168.95]. At target 0.90, they were 478.03 and 312.89 s, with difference −165.15 s [−177.00, −153.57]. These are within-model reductions of approximately 34–43 percent across the six no-yielding load targets, rather than calibrated predictions for a city.

The ordinary-traffic trade-off changed with loading. The M2 signed extra post-dispatch residence was −0.025 vehicle-hours [−0.116, 0.065] at target 0.60, 0.333 [0.060, 0.605] at 0.80, 1.349 [0.857, 1.873] at 0.90 and 1.663 [1.108, 2.259] at 0.95. These are restricted-horizon effects that include external source waiting and unfinished ordinary trips. The low-load interval is compatible with either a modest benefit or a modest cost; it does not establish zero cost. The increasing high-load point estimates motivate load-sensitive control, but do not establish a universal inverse-slack law or a transferable threshold.

The mechanical changes affected both the ambulance baseline and the priority trajectories. At target 0.90, M2 mean ambulance time increased from 291.57 to 312.89 s. More importantly, its baseline-paired ordinary cost rose from 0.964 to 1.349 vehicle-hours. The matched difference of effects, (corrected M2 − corrected M0) − (original M2 − original M0), was 0.384 vehicle-hours [0.125, 0.646]. At target 0.95, the corresponding increase was 0.190 [−0.090, 0.478]. Thus the effect of correcting mechanics is not captured by adding the ambulance's own travel-time penalty, and its direction and precision depend on the operating condition.

## Policy contrasts and residual risk

Phase skipping produced a clear ambulance-time gain over extension/truncation in the corrected model. At target 0.80, M2 − M1 was −86.49 s [−95.88, −77.17]; its ordinary-residence contrast was 0.088 vehicle-hours [−0.176, 0.350]. The latter interval does not establish equal ordinary cost. The queue-trigger and longer-hold family MQ did not show a clear ambulance improvement over M2 at target 0.90: the difference was 1.49 s [−4.38, 7.48], while extra ordinary residence increased by 0.438 vehicle-hours [0.141, 0.754]. Because this family changes trigger parameters and hold cap together, that comparison cannot identify the isolated causal contribution of queue awareness. Adding rerouting to the compensated family at the same target gave MR − MC = −10.15 s [−18.39, −3.50] and −0.261 vehicle-hours [−0.526, −0.031]. These condition-specific intervals are descriptive and are not simultaneous confidence bands.

Long ambulance trips remained frequent near high load despite the mean benefit. Under M2 without yielding, no trip exceeded 300 s among 200 scenarios at target 0.60; the corresponding two-sided 95 percent binomial upper endpoint is 1.83 percent. The observed fraction was 22.5 percent at target 0.80, 41.0 percent at 0.90 and 42.5 percent at 0.95. The 300 s threshold is an experimental summary rather than a clinical deadline. The findings support testing selective and route-aware policies under repeated missions, while leaving the realized time–aftereffect trade-off to the separate microscopic evaluation.

## Independent analytic verification

A separate initially empty, constant-arrival queue calculation was checked by independent piecewise-linear integration. For service capacity 0.5 vehicle/s and a 10 s service blackout, exact additional residence at loads 0.50, 0.70, 0.85 and 0.90 is 25.00, 58.33, 141.67 and 225.00 vehicle-seconds. Integration discrepancies were below 3×10^−12 vehicle-seconds. A deterministic fine-packet reference converged under refinement; at numerical packet mass 0.005 vehicle-equivalents, relative area errors were 0.215–0.300 percent. These calculations verify the stated isolated-queue expression and numerical accounting. They do not establish a network cost law or substitute for measured-corridor validation.
