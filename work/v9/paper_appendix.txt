# Supplementary methods

## Identification and interpretation of the experiment

Each policy comparison is a comparison within an explicitly specified structural simulation model. Ordinary demand, vehicle class, intended ordinary route and mission schedules are fixed across policies. Signal commands, ambulance route choices, queue evolution and realized vehicle trajectories are allowed to differ. Thus paired differences identify the modeled effect of changing the controller under that shared episode. They do not identify a causal effect in a real city without evidence that the model reproduces the relevant intervention responses.

The generated nominal-load axis scales demand against assigned PCU discharge and a designed green split. It is useful for controlled stress comparisons, but is not a measured volume-to-capacity ratio in the microscopic plant. Observed accumulation, throughput and boundary queues must accompany its interpretation. A network can have a high design ratio on one approach while retaining substantial storage and route alternatives elsewhere. The same index can represent a different physical regime after changing lane discipline, signal compatibility or headways.

Paired scenarios do not imply that every policy-dependent random event is coupled by the same vehicle identity. In the mesoscopic yielding model, stream draws are consumed at policy-dependent queue events. The original generator is retained for the matched reanalysis; this coupling limitation is part of its scope. In the microscopic extension, speed factors and car-following imperfection are fixed, while generated arrivals, class and route draws and observation-noise streams are explicitly namespaced. The same observation error is applied to each policy's own count, rather than copying a baseline count into the intervention trajectory.

The experiment's aftereffect window includes ongoing ordinary demand until the common end. It therefore measures a finite traffic operating period, including post-mission evolution, rather than stopping all arrivals and waiting for an empty network. Terminal ordinary vehicles and positive spatial differences can remain. The terminal spatial L1 difference describes that remaining state discrepancy, not an estimate of the time needed to recover. A future experiment can add a declared extended horizon or an arrival-shutoff clearance experiment, but those answer different operational questions and should not replace the present estimand silently.

## Development history and version control

The first microscopic implementation was tested for vehicle census, residence reconciliation, signal timing and route feasibility. A development pilot identified that a request could be reconsidered after its successful green made its predicted benefit disappear. The execution rule was changed to retain an admitted request through a bounded approach lease, and the resulting source was frozen for the initial parameter grid. That grid used 12 episodes and 14 configurations, including the two reference controllers, producing 168 runs. All selective candidates exceeded the declared 20 s development tolerance.

A subsequent development version added irreversible per-ambulance escalation based on observed free-flow progress. Its targeted pilot used an already designated development episode. The second grid used the same 12 development episodes, two reference controllers and nine selective configurations, producing 132 runs. This is adaptive method development, not preregistration. The first source and all failed development results are retained. The final model and selected parameter file were frozen before the separate microscopic evaluation. The selected configuration is documented with its source hash and the machine-readable protocol.

The mesoscopic and microscopic policy identifiers are intentionally distinct in the manuscript. M0, M1, M2, MQ, MC and MR refer to the original six-policy family. S0, SEQ, JFAST and JSEL refer to the new microscopic family, whose executable labels are P0, P1, P2 and P3. JSEL includes the progress-based switch. The no-switch variant appears only in the initial development results and the declared ablation. “Queue-aware,” “joint” and “selective” describe the specific implemented rules, rather than generic categories with interchangeable guarantees.

## Replication and software availability

The accompanying archive contains the original supplied mesoscopic source and results, the corrected mesoscopic source with a mechanical patch, the frozen initial and final microscopic implementations, synthetic input tables, statistical scripts, experiment protocols, selected parameters, verification records, episode summaries and population traces. Ordinary individual event files allow the traveler outcome and the residence identity to be reconstructed. Dependency versions and commands are specified in the archive's README. The environment-specific installation directory is excluded.

Every completed microscopic episode records the scenario hash, model configuration, mission-level dispatch and arrival information, traffic outcomes and numerical diagnostics. The batch stops on an exception instead of discarding a failed run. All generated missions remain in the ledger. Policy-independent schedule hashes are compared across each four-policy evaluation group. The same baseline population history is used to compute positive spatial exposure for each policy in that group. Summary tables are generated from these records rather than transcribed from simulator screen displays.

The numerical comparison at 0.25 s uses the same integer-second desired-release grid and one-second controller decisions as the primary 0.5 s experiment. This comparison tests sensitivity to physical integration and vehicle-following updates, not sensitivity to a twice-as-frequent decision-maker. Differences in treatment contrasts are analyzed as paired seed effects. A finite-step difference can change individual lane assignments and vehicle order; convergence of every trajectory is not assumed.

## Remaining algorithmic research requirements

The current route-action library is deliberately small. A stronger controller should represent uncertain queue position and downstream receiving conditions, simulate the signal automaton in its forecasts, and evaluate joint requests when several ambulances compete. An appropriate next comparison is a matched receding-horizon optimizer using exactly the same observations, phase transitions, receiving limits and computation budget. Simply labeling a comparator “MPC” without specifying its prediction dynamics and feasible controls would not establish a fair benchmark. A genuine pressure-based comparator additionally requires downstream turn-weighted queues and a declared treatment of finite storage, minimum greens and clearances.

The progress switch protects against a detected failure of the selective forecast, but it can turn the controller into aggressive priority early in a mission. Its activation frequency must therefore be reported. If most missions escalate, favorable time results cannot be credited solely to successful selective optimization. An uncertainty-aware extension should decide when confidence is sufficient to withhold a request, and test whether that decision improves both time and ordinary-traveler effects. Such an extension should be evaluated on fresh scenarios after selection; it is not an unimplemented benefit of the present experiment.

A city-scale implementation requires measured end-to-end processing times, fault handling and data-availability tests across much larger graphs. The present district does not establish network-wide scalability or universal transferability. Basic signal legality and numerical conservation are necessary checks, but do not demonstrate safe road operation. The intended long-term system remains a joint route-and-signal controller with explicit recovery objectives; the contribution of this draft is a reproducible, falsifiable first empirical step toward that system.
