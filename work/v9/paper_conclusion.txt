This study produces a reproducible first empirical implementation of joint ambulance routing and selective signal priority with explicit ordinary-traffic aftereffect accounting. Independent reproduction of the supplied model exposed ambulance storage and service exceptions that materially changed estimated congestion costs. Correcting those mechanics and executing 14,400 mesoscopic runs preserved the ambulance benefit while showing increased ordinary costs at higher nominal loading.

The separate 1,728-run microscopic evaluation supports the selected controller's balanced 20 s time-tolerance target relative to aggressive joint control, together with lower positive spatial exposure. Positive individual restricted-residence effects also decreased. These findings establish a within-model average trade-off, not a best-possible city route or a per-mission performance guarantee. Two selective-policy trips lasting more than 30 minutes demonstrate the remaining need for stronger prediction and stalled-route handling.

The code, inputs, protocols, raw episode records, individual event ledgers and numerical checks make the findings inspectable and repeatable. The next empirical step is corridor calibration and intervention-response validation, followed by matched comparison with a stronger receding-horizon optimizer. The long-term objective remains ambulance-time optimization with selective priority and minimal recovery costs; this draft advances that objective with actual computation while stating precisely where the evidence ends.
