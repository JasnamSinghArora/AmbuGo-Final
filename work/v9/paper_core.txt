# Joint ambulance routing and selective signal priority under repeated emergency demand

# Introduction

Emergency traffic control has two coupled responsibilities. It must shorten the time required for an ambulance to reach its destination, and it must limit the congestion created by changing service at intersections. Choosing a route from current link speeds and then requesting green along that route can overlook a fundamental interaction: the signal intervention changes the travel times on which the route choice was based. Conversely, a route that appears slower under ordinary control may become preferable when a small number of well-timed priority requests can clear its critical approaches. The decision is therefore a joint route-and-control problem, with congestion recovery forming part of its objective.

The consequences for ordinary traffic extend beyond the directly interrupted approach. A queue can consume downstream storage, block an upstream intersection, displace service to another movement, or delay vehicles that enter after the ambulance has passed. A network total may conceal these spatial effects: improvements in one part of the district can offset additional occupancy elsewhere. Measurement must also include vehicles waiting to enter the simulated network. Otherwise, a controller can appear successful merely because congestion has been displaced outside the accounting boundary. These observations motivate a fixed-population, fixed-horizon comparison of complete controlled trajectories.

This study develops and evaluates a causal controller that jointly considers a small route library and selective signal requests. Its central design is an explicit preference for small predicted congestion effects among routes and actions that remain within a specified predicted-time band. Requests are executed through a signal automaton that retains minimum greens and clearance stages, and competing ambulances are arbitrated explicitly. A queue-inspired burden score makes selectivity responsive to local loading. Because that score is approximate, its mathematical selection property is evaluated separately from realized ambulance travel and ordinary-traffic effects.

The empirical work has two complementary layers. First, a supplied synthetic mesoscopic implementation and its archived experiment are independently reproduced and mechanically corrected. This layer provides a broad comparison of conventional extension, phase skipping, queue-aware triggering, compensation and rerouting. Second, a new microscopic implementation uses physical vehicle following and lane changing in Eclipse SUMO and evaluates joint routing, selective priority and repeated missions. The microscopic experiment shares the district geometry with the mesoscopic model, but has separately specified demand, sensing and control. Agreement across these layers is therefore evidence about robustness of a mechanism, rather than an exact cross-simulator replication of numerical effect sizes.

The research questions are: how much does joint route-and-priority selection change ambulance travel relative to route-only and sequential controllers; how do ordinary residence and spatial occupancy differences vary with loading and mission dose; and can a selective rule retain a declared ambulance-time tolerance while reducing aftereffects? These questions are tested with paired scenarios, disjoint development and evaluation seeds, explicit unfinished-trip accounting, and archived executable code. Numerical checks establish properties of the implementations. Observed-corridor calibration and real-city predictive validation remain separate research stages.

# Mesoscopic experiment and mechanical reanalysis

The mesoscopic district contains 16 signalized junctions, eight boundary nodes and 59 directed links. Links have one or two lanes, lengths approximately 260–472 m, free-flow speeds approximately 30 or 40 km/h, and assumed saturation flows of 0.40–0.48 passenger-car units per second per lane. Cars, motorcycles, autorickshaws, buses and goods vehicles have probabilities 0.53, 0.30, 0.10, 0.04 and 0.03, and respective PCU factors 1, 0.5, 1, 3 and 2.5. These are synthetic design assumptions rather than estimates for a particular city.

Each non-exit link has PCU storage equal to the integer part of 0.16 times its lane count times its usable length in metres, with a 5 m stop-line buffer. One FIFO deque exposes its first one or two vehicles as possible discharge heads according to the declared lane count. Shared approach service credit is accumulated in PCU, and accepted ordinary departures debit their PCU requirement. A free-flow readiness clock is assigned at link entry. This is a pooled-head queue approximation rather than a lane-resolved model: physical lane allocation, turning pockets, longitudinal vehicle packing and lateral gaps are not represented. Exit links act as unbounded free-flow transport delays; their vehicles leave the explicit deque but remain in the completion ledger until their scheduled exit. Desired boundary arrivals wait in external origin FIFOs, whose residence contributes to the ordinary population ledger.

The nominal signal plan serves one protected incoming approach at a time, followed by 3 s amber and 2 s all-red. An exclusive pedestrian reservation comprises 7 s walk, 16 s clearance and 2 s all-red; no individual pedestrian trajectories are simulated. Approach discharge is permitted after a 2 s green start-up loss and during amber, giving the design effective-green duration g+1 s for a nominal green g. Webster-inspired cycles are clipped to 60–150 s, demand-proportional splits are constrained to 8–60 s greens, and actual integer stage lengths determine the realized cycle. The design degree of saturation is assigned mean PCU flow divided by nominal approach capacity, using this effective-green convention. The reported load values are targets; integer timing gives an achieved maximum of 0.79499 for the 0.80 target, while the other targets differ by less than 0.0002. This is an ex ante design index rather than measured traffic saturation under incidents and guard interventions. All policies share a stage-selection deadline guard. Priority policies additionally apply a per-tick protection guard during active priority and for two nominal cycles after release. These guards provide service opportunities and do not guarantee a wait limit for every queued vehicle.

Origin demand rates have fixed relative weights drawn uniformly between 230 and 390 vehicles per hour, then multiplied by a common design scaling factor. Origin-minute counts are modulated by a common stationary Gaussian AR(1) state with coefficient 0.75; its lognormal demand multiplier has log standard deviation 0.18 and unit expectation. Conditional release times are uniform within each minute. The count generator uses exact Poisson draws below a mean of 30 and a nonnegative rounded-normal approximation above that threshold. The approximation was not invoked in the held-out evaluation: the largest origin-minute mean was 14.608. Destination weights are proportional to exp(−distance/2200 m), multiplied by attraction factors 1.6 for the hospital boundary BE1, 1.2 for BE2 and BS1, and 1 for other destinations. Ordinary trips choose among up to four loop-free routes within 1.25 times the shortest free-flow time, with logit sensitivity 0.05 per second. Ordinary routes remain fixed after release.

An incident occurs with probability 0.5 per episode on one randomly selected two-lane non-exit link. It starts between 180 s before and 120 s after ambulance dispatch and lasts 240–480 s. One lane retains 45–65 percent of its discharge capacity, giving a whole-approach capacity multiplier between 0.725 and 0.825. A speed multiplier between 0.75 and 0.90 modifies readiness clocks for vehicles entering while the incident is active; vehicles already traveling retain their previously assigned clock. Cameras sample non-exit-link vehicle counts every 5 s, with no additional receipt delay. Their noise probabilities are 3/7 at zero and 1/7 each at −2, −1, 1 and 2, followed by clipping at zero. At each sampling instant, availability transitions from available to missing with probability 0.015 and back with probability 0.25. With probability 0.5, an additional 180–420 s outage affects approaches within an 800 m Manhattan-distance cluster around a sampled junction. The yielding sensitivity reorders vehicles ahead of the ambulance once it reaches a queue, using probabilities rho times 0.6, 1.0, 0.8, 0.2 and 0.2 for the five classes in the stated order. This conserves aggregate occupancy but does not establish a physically feasible rescue lane. Yield draws use a sequential stream consumed by policy-dependent encounters, so shared seeds do not imply identical vehicle-specific yielding decisions across policies.

The six mesoscopic policies are denoted M0, M1, M2, MQ, MC and MR to distinguish them from the microscopic controllers. M0 gives no ambulance signal priority. M1 uses a 30 s free-flow look-ahead, extends the ambulance green by at most 10 s and truncates conflicting green to its 8 s minimum without phase skipping. M2 uses the same look-ahead, permits a jump after required clearance and holds the ambulance green up to 60 s, subject to protection guards. A pedestrian reservation already in progress is not interrupted, and a walk-start age above 120 s prevents discretionary pedestrian skipping. MQ modifies the trigger using queue-clearance estimates, with its inherited 15 s look-ahead threshold, a 10 s margin and a 90 s green cap. Thus MQ versus M2 changes both triggering and the hold cap; it is a controller-family contrast rather than a clean isolation of queue awareness. MC adds compensation based on nominal service-share deficits, constrained by guard slack. MR adds estimated-queue-aware ambulance rerouting at link entries, switching only for a predicted gain exceeding 10 s. Initial ambulance routes are free-flow shortest paths. These policies correspond to P0, P1, P2, P3, P3C and P4 in the mesoscopic source package.

The archived evaluation has six nominal load targets, 0.60, 0.70, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90 and 0.95; two yielding parameters, 0 and 0.5; six policies; and 200 seeds per cell, giving 14,400 policy runs. Within each cell, ordinary releases, destinations, classes, routes, incidents and the ambulance mission are matched across policies. The same seed indices are reused across load and yielding conditions. Each episode begins empty and has one ambulance dispatched at an integer time between 1,800 and 2,100 s from one of six boundary origins to BE1. The common simulation horizon is 4,200 s, so post-dispatch follow-up ranges from 2,100 to 2,400 s. The 1,800 s initialization period is retained from the supplied design without treating it as proof of stationarity. Original tuning used seeds 5000–5039 at load targets 0.80 and 0.90 under both yielding regimes. The selected policy parameters are held fixed throughout mechanical reanalysis.

Source inspection identified three ambulance-specific mechanical exceptions. The ambulance could enter a full receiving link, could cross without sufficient discharge credit and without consuming that credit, and was inserted 20 m downstream of its origin without source-admission waiting. The original storage audit explicitly permitted the ambulance's PCU above declared storage. The corrected implementation removes these exceptions: desired dispatch joins the source FIFO once; insertion obeys source PCU service and strict receiving storage, begins at position zero, and uses the same stop-line discharge-credit rule as ordinary vehicles. Travel time still starts at desired dispatch, so admission waiting is included. Ordinary conservation excludes a queued ambulance from ordinary counts. No policy, demand, ordinary route-choice or signal-design code is changed. Exit-link transport and the remaining mesoscopic approximations are retained.

The corrected experiment repeats all 14,400 policy runs with the original parameter choices. It is a matched mechanical sensitivity analysis of the supplied experiment rather than an independently preregistered trial. Its purpose is to quantify how the identified admission, storage and service assumptions change the model's ambulance benefits and baseline-paired ordinary-traffic effects.

# Experimental design and statistical analysis

The microscopic development stage uses separate scenario seeds from the evaluation stage. Development assesses the trigger/action library, local burden threshold and predicted-time tolerance. It compares each selective candidate with the aggressive joint controller using the same generated episode. The selection criterion is an episode-mean ambulance-time increase of at most 20 s; among candidates satisfying that condition in development, the minimum mean positive spatial occupancy exposure is preferred. Twenty seconds is an explicit research design tolerance, not a medical standard. Its satisfaction in a training sample does not establish population noninferiority.

The microscopic evaluation varies nominal loading and scheduled ambulance dose. All policies share ordinary desired releases, classes, fixed ordinary routes, ambulance origins and dispatch times. Missions within an episode can overlap and are not treated as statistically independent observations. The same scenario seed is also reused across nominal-load and dose conditions; aggregate inference therefore resamples complete seed blocks rather than treating every condition as independent. The control code and selected configuration are frozen before the evaluation batch. Development diagnostics and parameter comparisons are retained separately from evaluation results.

The primary ambulance outcome is each episode's mean travel time from desired dispatch, including insertion waiting. A common-horizon restricted value and a fixed 600 s restricted outcome are retained for incomplete missions. We report completion and the fraction exceeding 300 and 600 s separately. These thresholds describe the synthetic experiment; they are not clinical service standards. All scheduled missions remain in the analysis, including any that never enter the road network or do not finish by the horizon.

Ordinary-traffic outcomes are restricted residence J, its signed paired change relative to route-only control, and the positive spatial occupancy exposure A on a fixed partition. The partition contains all regular links, a shared internal-junction group and the external source queues. The difference between two controllers' A values compares how much positive spatial accumulation each creates relative to the same baseline; it is distinct from directly integrating the positive difference between those two controllers. A includes moving as well as stopped vehicles, and can be influenced by shifts in vehicle order and location. It must not be read as a count of harmed people or as exclusively queueing delay.

Within each experimental condition, 95 percent percentile-bootstrap intervals are calculated from 10,000 resamples of paired scenario differences. Balanced overall effects first average each seed's differences over the full condition grid, then resample seeds. Mission-weighted and episode-weighted estimands are distinguished. The primary overall weighting assigns equal importance to each load–dose condition; it does not represent an assumed city's frequency of those conditions. Bootstrap intervals have approximate finite-sample coverage, and condition-specific intervals are descriptive rather than simultaneous confidence bands.

The joint research target requires both an ambulance-time difference below the prespecified tolerance and a reduction in positive exposure. We assess the corresponding effect intervals and report whether the data support each requirement, rather than declaring success from a non-significant difference. A confidence interval containing zero is described as inconclusive at the observed precision. The mesoscopic sign-flip calculations are independently reconstructed, but are not used as an exact test of a general mean-zero null: independent sign reversal requires additional exchangeability or symmetry assumptions. Multiple-test adjustments cannot repair invalid underlying null assumptions.

Rare-event summaries are computed at the episode level when missions share a scenario. A zero observed failure count is accompanied by a finite upper confidence bound, not a claim of zero failure probability. The original single-mission mesoscopic binomial intervals remain interpretable under independent generated seeds within a fixed cell. They quantify uncertainty within the assumed generator, rather than uncertainty about real-world collision or operational risk.

Numerical sensitivity changes the microscopic integration step while retaining the same control decision period and desired-release grid. Controller and simulator runtimes are recorded separately from file writing and statistical analysis where available. Runtime measurements describe the present local experiment; they do not establish a city-scale real-time guarantee. The initialization period is reported without assuming stationary traffic. Population trajectories, external waiting and unfinished vehicles are inspected to distinguish a finite-horizon intervention effect from background accumulation.

# Discussion

The central distinction is between a well-defined decision problem, a computationally tractable policy and empirical evidence about that policy. A finite route/action library permits explicit enumeration and reproducible comparisons, but the route estimates are forecasts. Their mathematical time-band property does not guarantee a corresponding realized ambulance-time bound. In particular, a priority decision changes the queue that subsequent observations measure, so a controller that reevaluates a successful request as unnecessary can withdraw it prematurely. Explicit bounded execution commitments are part of the method, rather than an implementation detail that can be omitted from the theory.

Congestion aftereffects require two kinds of accounting. Signed residence captures whether ordinary vehicles collectively remain in the modeled system longer over the observation horizon. Positive spatial exposure captures increases that may be offset by decreases elsewhere. Neither alone is a complete social objective. The first can hide spatial redistribution; the second depends on the chosen spatial partition and can register trajectory rearrangement without a comparable net travel-time loss. Reporting both, alongside ambulance time and unfinished trips, makes the trade-off visible without assigning unsupported passenger occupancies or monetary values.

The isolated-queue derivation explains why low spare capacity can amplify a service interruption. It does not establish a universal network threshold or inverse-slack law. The duration and location of an intervention, downstream storage, platoon timing, route redundancy, phase compatibility and demand heterogeneity all influence the realized response. The study's nominal load index is a controlled design variable. Its numerical thresholds cannot be transferred directly to another city or to another signal phase plan.

Several ambulances introduce both interaction and allocation. Their effects need not equal the single-mission effect multiplied by the number of missions. Successive requests may share a useful green, interrupt recovery, or compete for incompatible approaches. Deterministic earliest-dispatch arbitration makes this interaction reproducible, but is not a clinical triage policy. A later extension should incorporate externally defined urgency and destination constraints, with an explicit fairness or deadline objective, rather than silently treating dispatch order as medically optimal.

The present method uses graph routing, state-dependent queue estimates and finite candidate control. More elaborate estimators or learned graph models should be judged by their ability to improve decisions on held-out interventions. The small benefit of substituting exact queues into one particular mesoscopic heuristic is not an upper bound on the value of information to every controller. Conversely, a sophisticated estimator is not justified solely by its name or by forecast accuracy on uncontrolled traffic. It must improve feasible route-and-priority decisions at an acceptable complete-pipeline computational cost. No GNN, Bayesian particle filter or reinforcement-learning advantage is claimed in this experiment.

# Limitations and the field calibration stage

All demand, geometry, vehicle parameters and sensor laws used here are synthetic. The original district is irregular and includes heterogeneous links and mixed classes, but realism of ingredients does not establish accuracy of their joint behavior. The microscopic model uses deterministic speed factors and car-following imperfection, while randomness enters the arrival, class, route and mission processes. It omits physical pedestrians, emergency yielding, continuous sublane filtering, curbside stopping, parking maneuvers and unannounced incidents. The mesoscopic layer includes stylized incidents and yielding, but those abstractions are not a substitute for microscopic or observed validation. Different mechanisms are therefore covered by different experimental layers.

Single-approach protected phases and an exclusive pedestrian reservation restrict available capacity. They allow a transparent clearance automaton, but are not representative of every junction. A service-age guard provides an opportunity for overdue approaches; it does not guarantee that a particular queued vehicle crosses within a fixed wait. The original mesoscopic waiting metric counts over-threshold discharge episodes and omits unfinished waits. It is retained only with that definition, not interpreted as a full deadline-violation probability. The microscopic service-age metric is likewise reported as a controller diagnostic.

The route library excludes many possible detours, and ordinary vehicles do not adapt their routes to priority-induced congestion. Replanning uses approximate current-state predictions rather than complete stochastic optimal control. No global optimum over all city routes, signal programs or future scenarios is established. The wider Counterfactual Cone Refinement framework developed in the preceding proposal is not implemented by these experiments and is not credited with their results. Its exact finite-domain certification remains a separate algorithmic research path.

Field calibration should begin with a single corridor and its receiving approaches. Required records include lane and turning-pocket geometry; permitted phase transitions and actual controller logs; turning counts by class; saturation discharge headways; queue tails and spillback periods; matched travel times; incident and sensor-availability logs; and anonymized ambulance dispatch, insertion, approach and arrival times. Video or trajectories are needed to calibrate yielding and lateral interactions. A nominal government data-access agreement does not itself supply these measurements or validate a parameter value.

Data should be split by day and operating condition into calibration and validation sets before controller comparison. Geometry and control logic should first reproduce the observed system; then demand, discharge, car-following and yielding parameters should be fitted against multiple observables. Validation must include queue lengths, travel-time distributions, spillback onset, approach discharge and responses to signal changes. Fitting only network mean speed cannot establish correct intervention response. The control policy should be evaluated on held-out periods and, eventually, on a distinct corridor. A controlled deployment study would require its own operational authorization and evidence design.

The supplied code, input schemas, hashes and raw episode summaries make the present experiments reproducible. They support a first empirical manuscript and a concrete data-collection specification. They do not yet establish real-city accuracy, deployable safety, clinical benefit or a universally superior priority policy.
