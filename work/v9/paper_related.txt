# Related work and contribution

## Emergency routing and signal control

Ambulance passage through a signalized network depends on both the route and the signals encountered along it. Selecting a route from current link speeds and subsequently requesting green can miss a route whose signals are easier to alter, while aggressive priority can displace congestion onto other approaches. This coupling is established in emergency traffic-control research. Hosseinzadeh and colleagues formulate emergency-centered multi-intersection control using model predictive control and a network traffic model, considering centralized and decentralized implementations [R1]. Their formulation also includes control of inlet flows. Comparisons must therefore distinguish traffic authority: a method allowed to meter network entry has decisions unavailable to a system restricted to ambulance routing and signal requests.

The present study addresses a narrower operational problem. It implements a causal controller that evaluates a finite library of route continuations and priority settings, applies a queue-inspired admission rule, and escalates individual ambulances to a stronger intervention mode when observed progress becomes poor. This is an integration of known control and modeling principles. Its contribution must be assessed through the resulting executable method, accounting and empirical tradeoff. The combination of routing and signal priority alone is not claimed as novel, and candidate selection under an approximate forecast is not claimed to produce the globally optimal route in a city.

Queue-responsive signal control provides relevant alternatives and cautionary results. Varaiya's max-pressure framework uses local queue information to control a specified store-and-forward network and derives throughput properties under explicit modeling assumptions [R3]. Capacity-aware back-pressure addresses finite storage, non-work-conservation and congestion propagation [R2]. These foundations motivate checking receiving conditions before spending conflicting green on an ambulance movement. They do not imply that any rule favoring a long queue is max-pressure. The longest-queue sensitivity in this study is named according to its actual implementation, and no max-pressure stability theorem is assigned to it.

Practical signal constraints further limit the transfer of idealized guarantees. A controller must preserve minimum service and clearance intervals while deciding which approach to serve. Recent analysis specifically examines the consequences of inter-green and minimum-green constraints for decentralized max-pressure stability [R14]. Thus a priority controller requires its own admissible signal automaton and transition checks. A maximum age between service opportunities also differs from a bound on an individual vehicle's queueing time: serving an approach does not ensure that every waiting vehicle can depart during that green.

## Traffic representation and urban aftereffects

Traffic-flow models provide a conservation structure for the control problem. The cell transmission model represents network traffic using vehicle conservation and sending and receiving restrictions [R15]. These restrictions explain why an ambulance green can have little value when the downstream link is full and why a local service reduction can propagate upstream. A passenger-car-unit queue model can explore such mechanisms efficiently, but its abstraction of physical space and heterogeneous discharge must be stated. Analytical agreement in an isolated queue is useful verification of that representation; it does not establish that the same model accurately predicts a particular urban corridor.

Microscopic simulation supplies a complementary experimental layer. SUMO represents individual vehicle movement, lane use and internal junction connections and supports external signal control [R4,R5]. Its documentation makes clear that direct signal-state commands leave phase and transition management to the application [R5]. The microscopic experiments therefore retain physical car following and receiving restrictions while separately auditing the signal automaton and ordinary-vehicle ledger. This challenges particular mesoscopic abstractions, including ambulance storage and service exceptions. Agreement across model layers would increase confidence in a mechanism under the tested assumptions; disagreement would identify sensitivity requiring explanation. Neither outcome alone constitutes external validation.

Mixed traffic introduces an additional modeling boundary. Vehicles differ in dimensions, acceleration and following behavior, while weak lane discipline and emergency yielding involve lateral space. Kanagaraj and Treiber explicitly model width-sensitive interactions in heterogeneous traffic [R16]. Passenger-car-unit conversion alone cannot reproduce those interactions. The primary microscopic study includes heterogeneous vehicle parameters but does not infer calibrated Indian yielding behavior from them. Siren response, lateral filtering and physical pedestrian interaction require observational evidence and additional validation before operational conclusions are extended to those behaviors.

Urban-scale traffic research also cautions against reducing congestion to one network average. Geroliminis and Daganzo report regional macroscopic flow-density relationships in a measured urban setting [R8]. Loder and colleagues relate differences in traffic capacity and critical accumulation to network characteristics across regional networks [R9]. These findings motivate recording accumulation, spatial distribution and network structure, but do not provide a universal saturation threshold for arbitrary synthetic networks. Nominal demand settings in this study remain design labels derived from assumed service parameters. Their achieved microscopic occupancy and throughput must be reported separately.

The aftereffect metrics follow this spatial interpretation. The signed integral of paired ordinary accumulation measures the difference in restricted residence over a common horizon. A second measure integrates positive occupancy differences over a fixed exhaustive partition, preventing occupancy reductions elsewhere from canceling local increases. The latter is a spatial exposure measure, not an estimate of every affected traveler's delay. Its algebraic decomposition and aggregation properties are elementary consequences of the positive-part operator. Their value here is reproducible measurement and error detection rather than the discovery of a new general traffic theorem.

## Forecast uncertainty and reproducible evaluation

A controller can satisfy an internal forecast criterion while missing the desired realized ambulance performance. The finite candidate library in this study supports a precise computed-time-band statement: selective-mode choices remain within the specified tolerance of the smallest forecast value evaluated at that snapshot. This statement is conditional on the forecast and finite library. Route hysteresis can retain an eligible incumbent with greater forecast burden, and future replanning changes both the information state and the candidate evaluation. The statement therefore supplies no guarantee of globally minimal harm, realized travel regret or recursive feasibility.

The observed-progress fallback addresses this gap through an explicit causal rule. Each ambulance accumulates credit only for executed regular-edge free-flow progress and compares that credit with time elapsed since desired dispatch. If its progress deficit exceeds the development-selected threshold, that ambulance irreversibly adopts the aggressive route-and-priority mode. Other ambulances retain their own modes, while all continue to share physical queues, legal signal transitions and request arbitration. The trigger is a measurable progress proxy rather than a calibrated prediction of the aggressive comparator's completion time. In particular, escalation at a threshold cannot guarantee that the final trip differs from that comparator by no more than the threshold. The fallback's merit is an empirical question requiring comparison with both unguarded selectivity and always-aggressive control.

Official microsimulation guidance distinguishes fitting observed traffic conditions from checking a model's code and from evaluating alternatives [R6,R7]. A real-corridor study would require synchronized counts, signal logs, discharge headways, queue or occupancy trajectories and ambulance dispatch and arrival records. Default simulator parameters are not substitutes for those data. The present paired simulations instead provide controlled comparisons within a synthetic structural model, with common demand and mission schedules, source waiting, unfinished trips and explicit failure records retained.

Inference likewise depends on the experimental unit. Several ambulances in one episode interact and must not be treated as independent replications. Bootstrap resampling is an established tool for sampling-distribution approximation [R10]; here complete scenario seeds are resampled to retain within-episode dependence. Exact binomial intervals require independent Bernoulli trials [R12], and Holm adjustment requires valid constituent tests [R11]. Monte Carlo permutation corrections prevent spuriously zero sampled p-values [R13], but they do not establish the exchangeability required by the underlying permutation scheme. Confidence intervals that include zero are therefore reported as uncertainty rather than proof of zero cost or equality.

The reproducible increment investigated in this paper is the integrated pipeline: a mechanically audited mesoscopic study, a separate microscopic implementation, exact cohort residence accounting, fixed-partition spatial exposure, finite-library joint selection and an observed-progress escalation rule, evaluated across traffic loading and interacting mission schedules. Conservation, queue recovery arguments, candidate enumeration and statistical resampling are established foundations. Their combination can support a useful computational contribution if the measured tradeoff survives matched comparisons and sensitivity checks. The present manuscript is a first computational draft; field calibration, independently observed intervention responses and broader network testing remain necessary before city-level performance or deployment readiness is established.
